Claims that quantum computers will beat classical computers at simulating materials are usually made for toy models, at an accuracy the authors choose. We ask the question for a real material, with the accuracy set by an experiment. Our target is the neutron-scattering spectrum S(q, ω) of the Kitaev magnet α-RuCl3, computed to the energy resolution of the instrument. We time exact classical simulations on clusters of up to 18 spins and test matrix-product-state (MPS) simulations on cylinders. We measure the Trotter error of the matching quantum circuits against exact results and give the circuits to a surface-code resource estimator. For three published models of the material, a fault-tolerant quantum computer becomes faster than exact simulation at N* ≈ 36–38 spins. For the model of Winter et al. it then needs 0.25 M physical qubits at a physical error rate of 10^-4, or 1.7 M at 10^-3. At this point both machines need about a century, so the crossover marks where neither is practical, not where quantum simulation becomes useful. Even the layout with the fewest qubits needs 29–34 thousand physical qubits at an error rate of 10^-4. That is 29–34 times the 1000-qubit target of India’s National Quantum Mission. The two-qubit error reported for the 25-qubit QpiAI-Indus system is 4 times above the surface-code threshold. We do not claim an advantage today. We state the assumptions behind every projection, and code and data will be made public.
