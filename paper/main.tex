\title{When Will Quantum Computers Beat Classical Simulation of a Real Quantum Magnet?
A Quantum-Classical Crossover for the Neutron Spectrum of \RuCl}

\begin{abstract}
Claims that quantum computers will beat classical computers at simulating
materials are usually made for toy models, at an accuracy the authors
choose. We ask the question for a real material, with the accuracy set by
an experiment. Our target is the neutron-scattering spectrum
$S(\mathbf q,\omega)$ of the Kitaev magnet \RuCl, computed to the energy
resolution of the instrument. We time exact classical simulations on
clusters of up to 18 spins and test matrix-product-state (MPS)
simulations on cylinders. We measure the Trotter error of the matching quantum circuits
against exact results and give the circuits to a surface-code resource
estimator. For \nSets{} published models of the material, a fault-tolerant
quantum computer becomes faster than exact simulation at
$N^*\approx\NstarRangeAll$ spins. For the model of \mainSet{} \emph{et al.}
it then needs \physQWinterB{} physical qubits at a physical error rate of
$10^{-4}$, or \physQWinterA{} at $10^{-3}$. At this point both machines need
about a century, so the crossover marks where neither is practical, not
where quantum simulation becomes useful. Even the layout with the fewest qubits
needs \indiaMinQloB--\indiaMinQhiB{} thousand physical qubits at an error
rate of $10^{-4}$. That is \indiaMinGapNQMloB--\indiaMinGapNQMhiB{} times
the 1000-qubit target of India's National Quantum Mission. The two-qubit
error reported for the 25-qubit QpiAI-Indus system is
\indiaQpiaiOverThreshold{} times above the surface-code threshold. We do
not claim an advantage today. We state the assumptions behind every
projection, and code and data will be made public.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
quantum advantage, resource estimation, Kitaev materials, neutron scattering, Trotter error
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\noindent\textbf{Plain-language summary.}
Neutron beams show how the tiny magnets inside a crystal move. To compare
these measurements with a model, we need a computer simulation, and it gets
exponentially harder as the simulated piece of crystal grows. We estimate
how large that piece must be before a future error-corrected quantum
computer would finish faster than a classical computer, using the real
accuracy of neutron experiments on the magnet \RuCl. The answer is a few
dozen magnetic atoms. Reaching it needs tens to hundreds of thousands of
physical qubits, far more than any machine built or planned so far.

\section{Introduction}

Simulating how quantum systems change in time is the use most often named
for quantum computers. Yet most studies of when quantum computers win use a
model system and an accuracy chosen by the authors. Yoshioka
\emph{et al.}~\cite{yoshioka2024} found the crossover point for
ground-state energies of two-dimensional models. Sun
\emph{et al.}~\cite{sun2026} did the same for the dynamics of the
mixed-field Ising model, against tensor-network and variational Monte Carlo
methods. Two things are still missing for a result a materials scientist
can use: a real material whose model cannot be solved exactly, and an
accuracy set by a real measurement rather than by a free parameter $\eps$.

\RuCl{} is the leading candidate for Kitaev's honeycomb spin
liquid~\cite{kitaev2006,jackeli2009,takagi2019}. Its inelastic neutron
scattering (INS) spectrum shows a broad continuum whose origin is
debated~\cite{banerjee2016,banerjee2017,do2017,winter2017}. Several models
of its magnetic interactions compete~\cite{winter2017,ran2017,laurell2020},
and exact calculations on small clusters are the usual way to compare them
with data~\cite{laurell2020}. This makes it a natural target for quantum
simulation, and a natural test of when such a simulation would pay off.

\textbf{Contributions.} (i) An accuracy target taken from the energy
resolution of INS. The time window, the total simulated time and the
allowed error all follow from this one number (Sec.~\ref{sec:target}).
(ii) The measured classical cost of computing $S(\mathbf q,\omega)$ exactly
on periodic clusters, including clusters with several ground states, and
a check of how fast MPS simulations need to grow
(Sec.~\ref{sec:classical}). (iii) Trotter errors measured on the spectrum
itself, for the same circuit whose gates we count, and a full surface-code
resource estimate (Sec.~\ref{sec:quantum}). (iv) The crossover size $N^*$, how much it
depends on our assumptions, and how far the hardware it needs is from
India's stated targets (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).

\section{Model and accuracy target}\label{sec:target}

We use the spin-1/2 $J_1$-$K$-$\Gamma$-$\Gamma'$-$J_3$ model on the
honeycomb lattice~\cite{rau2014,winter2017},
\begin{multline}
H=\sum_{\langle ij\rangle_\gamma}\Big[J_1\,\mathbf S_i\!\cdot\!\mathbf S_j
+K S_i^\gamma S_j^\gamma
+\Gamma\big(S_i^\alpha S_j^\beta+S_i^\beta S_j^\alpha\big)\\
+\Gamma'\!\!\sum_{\mu=\alpha,\beta}\!\big(S_i^\gamma S_j^\mu+S_i^\mu S_j^\gamma\big)\Big]
+J_3\!\!\sum_{\langle\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle\rangle}\!\!\mathbf S_i\!\cdot\!\mathbf S_j,
\end{multline}
where $\gamma\in\{x,y,z\}$ labels the bond and $\alpha,\beta$ are the other
two axes. Our main parameter set is the minimal model of Winter
\emph{et al.}~\cite{winter2017}, fitted to ab initio results and neutron
data: $(J_1,K,\Gamma,J_3)=(-0.5,-5.0,2.5,0.5)$\,meV (as listed
in~\cite{maksimov2020}). Two more sets test how much the model matters: the
$K$-$\Gamma$ model of Ran \emph{et al.},
$(K,\Gamma)=(-6.8,9.5)$\,meV~\cite{ran2017}, and the set that Laurell and
Okamoto fitted to the specific heat~\cite{laurell2020},
$(J_1,K,\Gamma,\Gamma',J_3)=(-1.3,-15.1,10.1,-0.12,0.9)$\,meV (as listed
in~\cite{meta2026}). Their energy scales are about two and three times
larger.

\textbf{Observable.} We compute
\begin{equation}
S(\mathbf q,\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\!\int_{-T}^{T}\!\!dt\,w(t)\,e^{i\omega t}
\frac1N\sum_{ij}e^{i\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j)}
\langle\mathbf S_i(t)\!\cdot\!\mathbf S_j(0)\rangle
\end{equation}
with $\hbar=1$ and energies in meV. An instrument with a Gaussian energy
resolution of full width $\delta E$ measures the true spectrum blurred by
that Gaussian. In time, this blurring is exactly the window
$w(t)=e^{-t^2/2\tau^2}$ with $\tau=2\sqrt{2\ln2}/\delta E$. So we only need
the correlations up to the time $T=\tau\sqrt{2\ln(1/c)}$, where the window
drops below $c=10^{-3}$. Sampling every $\Delta t=\pi/\omega_{\max}$ covers
energies up to $\omega_{\max}=\omegaMax$\,meV. We take $\delta E=\dE$\,meV
as a typical high-resolution value; Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} shows
coarser ones. This gives $T=\Tmev$\,meV$^{-1}=\Tps$\,ps and \nTimes{} time
points (the rough estimate $2\pi\hbar/\delta E$ gives \TroughPs\,ps). We
require the blurred spectrum $\sum_a S^{aa}(\mathbf q,\omega)$ to be correct
to $\eps=\epsPct\%$ of its peak, a typical experimental uncertainty
(Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} covers 1--10\%). Half of this error budget goes
to Trotter error and half to shot noise.

\section{Classical cost}\label{sec:classical}

\textbf{Clusters.} For each $N$ we list all periodic clusters of $N/2$ unit
cells and pick the most compact one in which every first- and
third-neighbour bond joins a different pair of sites. Third-neighbour bonds
only work this way for $N\ge14$. We use $N=\exactSizesWinter$ for the
\mainSet{} set and $N=\exactSizesRan$ for the other two; larger clusters
are left for future work.

\textbf{Method.} The model conserves no spin component, so we work with the
full space of $2^N$ states and apply the Hamiltonian without storing it.
We find the ground state with the Lanczos method. Symmetric clusters have
several ground states: \degenWinterNFourteen{} for $N=14$ (three zigzag
directions times time reversal) and \degenWinterNEighteen{} for $N=18$ with
the \mainSet{} parameters. We find all of them and average over them,
because a single one breaks the translation symmetry. We evolve each
$S_j^b|0\rangle$ in time with the short-iterative Lanczos
method~\cite{park1986}, with an error of at most $10^{-9}$ per step, and
read off all correlations at each time. Translation symmetry means one
reference site $j$ per sublattice is enough. We tested the code on the
exactly solvable Kitaev model~\cite{kitaev2006,baskaran2007}, where it
reproduces the conserved plaquettes, the correlations that vanish beyond
nearest neighbours, and the ground-state energy to within 2\%. For $N=8$,
the spectrum agrees with an exact eigenstate calculation to $10^{-4}$ of
its peak.

\textbf{Measured cost.} The full spectrum takes \tExactWinterNFourteen,
\tExactWinterNSixteen{} and \tExactWinterNEighteen{} on a 4-core
workstation for $N=14$, 16 and 18, counting all ground states. Per ground
state the cost grows as $N^2 2^N$: each product with the Hamiltonian costs
about $N2^N$, and the number of products grows as $N$. A free exponential
fit gives a growth factor of \classBaseWinter{} per spin.
Fig.~\ref{fig:spectrum} shows the spectrum for $N=18$.

\textbf{Matrix product states.} We also evolve $S^z_j|0\rangle$ with
two-site TDVP~\cite{haegeman2016} in TeNPy~\cite{hauschild2018}, starting
from a DMRG ground state, on cylinders with a circumference of $W=2$ and 3
unit cells (16 and 18 spins). The MPS code uses the same bonds as the exact
code, and at full bond dimension the two agree to $10^{-9}$. For each bond
dimension $\chi$ we find the longest time window $T'$ for which the error
of the blurred spectrum stays below $\eps$. We bound this error by summing
the errors of all site pairs. For $W=2$, $\chi=16$, 32, 64 and 128 reach
$T'=\mpsReachWinterWTwoChiSixteen$, \mpsReachWinterWTwoChiThirtyTwo,
\mpsReachWinterWTwoChiSixtyFour{} and
\mpsReachWinterWTwoChiOneTwentyEight\,meV$^{-1}$ (the full window). For
$W=3$, $\chi=32$ and 64 reach only \mpsReachWinterWThreeChiThirtyTwo{} and
\mpsReachWinterWThreeChiSixtyFour\,meV$^{-1}$. A fit of
$\ln\chi=c_W+s_WT'$ gives growth rates $s_W=\mpsSWinterWTwo$ and
\mpsSWinterWThree\,meV. The needed bond dimension thus grows quickly with
time, and faster on the wider cylinder: even $W=3$ would need
$\chi\approx\mpsChiWThreeWinter$ for the full window, while the cost per
step grows as $\chi^3$. For the long window that the neutron resolution
demands, MPS is therefore no cheaper than exact simulation for the sizes we
study, and we use exact simulation as the classical baseline.

\section{Quantum cost}\label{sec:quantum}

\textbf{Circuit.} We use one qubit per spin and one extra (ancilla) qubit.
Each correlation is measured with the Hadamard test. The ancilla controls
only two Pauli gates, and the time evolution between them runs without
control~\cite{somma2002}. So the time evolution is the only part that needs
costly non-Clifford gates. It is a second- or fourth-order Suzuki product
formula~\cite{suzuki1991} built from the Pauli terms of $H$. Each term is a
rotation $e^{-i\theta\sigma^a_i\sigma^b_j}$. There are five such rotations
per nearest-neighbour bond for the \mainSet{} set (nine if $\Gamma'\neq0$)
and three per third-neighbour bond. We group them by bond type and merge
neighbouring rotations about the same term.

\textbf{Trotter error, measured.} We simulate exactly this sequence of
rotations on a state vector and form the correlations as the Hadamard test
would, evolving both $|0\rangle$ and $S^b_j|0\rangle$ with the circuit. We
then compare the blurred spectrum with the exact one for
$N=\exactSizesWinter$, for several step sizes $\delta t$ and three
resolutions. The error follows $\eps_{\rm Trot}=A\,T\,\delta t^{p}$, with
$p=\trotPWinter$ for second order (\trotNptsWinter{} points). Fitting an
extra factor $N^\alpha$ gives $\alpha=\trotFreeAlphaWinter$, so the error
does not grow with $N$. We use the largest prefactor among the sizes we
measured, $A=\trotAWinter$. This is what we expect for a quantity that does
not grow with system size and is limited by the resolution. Worst-case
commutator bounds~\cite{childs2021} would give far larger numbers.

\textbf{Shots.} Each shot gives $\pm1$, so after $n$ shots the variance of a
correlation of spin-1/2 operators is at most $1/16n$. Carried through the
windowed transform, this gives
$\mathrm{Var}\,S\le 3N\sum_k (w_k\Delta t)^2/(32\pi^2 n)$. We choose $n$ so
that two standard deviations stay below half of $\eps S_{\max}$. Here
$S_{\max}$ is the peak of the inelastic spectrum ($\omega>2\delta E$). It
leaves out the weight near zero energy that builds up at the zigzag
ordering wavevector on clusters that fit this order, and it takes the
smallest peak over the sizes we measured. For the \mainSet{} set,
$S_{\max}=\SpeakWinter$\,meV$^{-1}$ and $n\approx\shotsWinter$. With one
reference site per sublattice, three spin components, and real and
imaginary parts, we need $12N$ circuits per time point, or
$\circuitsWinterA$ in total at $N=\NstarWinterA$. All are independent and
could run on many machines at once. We compare one quantum computer with
one classical workstation.

\textbf{Error correction.} We pass the logical counts (qubits, rotations,
and rotation depth from scheduling each rotation as early as possible) to
Microsoft's resource estimator~\cite{beverland2022}. We use a surface
code~\cite{fowler2012}, gate-based qubits with nanosecond gates, physical
error rates $p=10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$, and a total error budget of 1\% per
circuit. The estimator includes rotation synthesis~\cite{ross2016} and
magic-state factories. One machine runs all circuits one after another.

\textbf{Ground-state preparation} is not included in these costs. Our exact
results show that the overlap of the ground state with the best product
state, optimised over all spin directions, falls roughly as
$e^{-\prepKappaWinter N}$ over $N=\exactSizesWinter$, so preparing the
ground state from a simple state could add a large cost; methods that need
fewer attempts~\cite{lin2020} reduce it.

Fig.~\ref{fig:crossover} shows the result for the \mainSet{} set. The
quantum cost grows only as a power of $N$, but it starts high. At $N^*$ one
circuit runs for seconds, and about $10^5$ circuits each need about $10^4$
shots. The curves cross at $N^*=\NstarRangeWinter$ for both error rates.
There the quantum computer needs \tQuantumWinterB{} ($p=10^{-4}$) or
\tQuantumWinterA{} ($p=10^{-3}$), against \tClassicalWinterB{} for the
classical computer (Table~\ref{tab:resources}). So the crossover tells us
about scaling, not about a useful calculation. A practical simulation at
$N^*$ needs much more parallel hardware and better algorithms on both
sides. Fourth-order formulas give the same $N^*$, because they need fewer
steps but five times as many rotations per step.

\textbf{Sensitivity.} Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} changes one assumption at
a time. Over all \gridCount{} combinations (three parameter sets, three
resolutions, four accuracies, four qubit types, and both Trotter orders
where measured), $N^*$ stays between \gridMin{} and \gridMax{} against one
workstation. \emph{Experiment:} a tighter accuracy or a finer resolution
moves $N^*$ by only a few spins. But it changes the quantum run time at
$N^*$ hugely, from years at $\delta E=2$\,meV to millennia at $\eps=1\%$,
because the number of shots grows as $\eps^{-2}$ and the circuits get
longer with $T$. \emph{Quantum hardware:} the physical error rate sets the
number of qubits but barely moves $N^*$. Slow gates (microseconds), as in
trapped ions, make each shot about 1500 times longer and give the largest
$N^*$ values. \emph{Classical hardware:} each doubling of classical speed
adds roughly one spin, so a large cluster $10^4$ times faster than our
workstation raises $N^*$ by about 14. \emph{Model:} the Ran \emph{et al.}
and Laurell and Okamoto sets (exact data for $N=\exactSizesRan$) have
Trotter prefactors $A=\trotARan$ and \trotALaurell, twenty to thirty times
that of the \mainSet{} set. Yet they cross over at nearly the same $N^*$,
because the number of steps grows only as $A^{1/2}$, and the circuit and
shot counts, which dominate, barely change.

\textbf{India roadmap.} The National Quantum Mission aims for machines with
50 to 1000 physical qubits within eight years~\cite{nqm2023}. The
superconducting QpiAI-Indus system has \indiaQpiaiQubits{} qubits with a
reported two-qubit fidelity of 96\%~\cite{qpiai2025}. We work out the
hardware needed at the crossover for all three parameter sets and two
surface-code layouts (Fig.~\ref{fig:india}). The fastest layout, used in
Table~\ref{tab:resources}, adds as many magic-state factories as speed up
the run. The smallest layout uses one factory. Each shot then takes 60 to
75 times longer, so the crossover comes later. \emph{Qubits.} At
$p=10^{-4}$ the crossover needs \indiaFastQloB--\indiaFastQhiB{} thousand
physical qubits in the fastest layout ($N^*=\indiaFastNloB$--\indiaFastNhiB)
and \indiaMinQloB--\indiaMinQhiB{} thousand in the smallest one
($N^*=\indiaMinNloB$--\indiaMinNhiB, but \indiaMinTloB--\indiaMinThiB{}
years of run time). At $p=10^{-3}$ these numbers become
\indiaFastQloA--\indiaFastQhiA{} and \indiaMinQloA--\indiaMinQhiA{}
thousand. The smallest of these machines is
\indiaMinGapNQMloB--\indiaMinGapNQMhiB{} times larger than the mission's
1000-qubit target. \emph{Error rate.} A
fidelity of 96\% means a 4\% error. This is \indiaQpiaiOverThreshold{} times
above the surface-code threshold of about 1\%, so no number of such qubits
can run this calculation. Reaching $p=10^{-3}$ or $10^{-4}$ needs an error
\indiaErrGapA{} or \indiaErrGapB{} times lower. The error rate is therefore
the main limit and comes first. Below threshold, each tenfold drop in $p$
cuts the qubit count by a factor of five to thirteen in our estimates,
which is worth more than adding qubits. For India's roadmap,
this application asks first for two-qubit errors near $10^{-4}$. Only then
does it ask for $10^4$ to $10^5$ qubits, well beyond the 2031 target.

\section{Limitations and outlook}

\emph{Classical baseline.} Exact simulation is not the strongest possible
classical method, so our $N^*$ is a lower limit on where an advantage could
start. Translation symmetry would save another factor of about $N/2$, and
continued-fraction Lanczos~\cite{gagliano1987} avoids time stepping
altogether for a fixed blurring. Our MPS test covers only two short
cylinders. We did not run PEPS, time-dependent variational Monte Carlo or
neural quantum states. Each could raise $N^*$, although long-time dynamics in two
dimensions is where they struggle most. \emph{Extrapolation.} We
extrapolate the classical costs and Trotter errors from $N\le18$ for the
\mainSet{} set and $N\le16$ for the others. Our claim that the Trotter error
does not grow with $N$ rests on few sizes with strong finite-size effects,
and so does the reference peak $S_{\max}$, which varies threefold between
sizes. \emph{Measurement.} Measuring $S(\mathbf q,t)$ directly instead of
correlations between site pairs, or sharing shots between circuits, could
cut the number of circuits by up to a factor of $N$. \emph{Physics.} We
assume zero temperature, ignore the neutron polarization factor, and use
parameter sets that are themselves uncertain. \emph{Ground-state
preparation} is left out and could add a large cost. \emph{Hardware.} We
have not yet run the circuits on a quantum device. Even so,
tying the accuracy to an experiment turns ``quantum advantage for
materials'' into a concrete number that others can check, and our code
lets each assumption be changed.
